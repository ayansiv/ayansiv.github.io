Monetary Policy Under Transformative AI

Research note. Last updated September 2026. Back to research

If AI raises growth quickly, the natural rate of interest moves with it, and every policy rule that leans on an estimate of that rate inherits the error. This note asks how large those errors could be and which rules fail first. It takes the three growth scenarios in Anthropic's Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI (Korinek, Jones, Sacher, Cotter and McCrory, 2026), runs them through a small New Keynesian model, and traces the path for the federal funds rate each one implies.

Central banks now say openly that AI could shift the neutral rate and blur measures of slack (see Lenzu, 2026, and a recent BIS speech), but mostly in qualitative terms and within modest growth ranges. The economists who take explosive growth seriously model real outcomes and leave money out. I haven't found anyone who puts calibrated transformative-AI scenarios through actual policy rules, so that's what this does.

Setup

I rebuilt the production side of the Anthropic model from their technical report: the task-based block, their capital supply schedule, and the ideas channel. Their labor market block is replaced with a simpler stand-in calibrated to their 2030 unemployment figures. The rebuild reproduces their 2030 results for GDP, growth, the return to capital and the labor share, exactly for the modest and substantial scenarios and to within about a point for the extreme one.

Their model has no sticky prices, so its output path is what New Keynesian models call natural output, and its unemployment is natural unemployment. That output path drives a standard quarterly New Keynesian model with a zero lower bound and an inertial Taylor rule, starting from where things stand today: a funds rate of 3.75 to 4 percent and core inflation near 3.4 percent.

The Anthropic model has no saving decision (their footnote 13 flags this), so it doesn't pin down the neutral rate. I use two versions. In the growth version, the neutral rate rises one for one with expected growth, as a household Euler equation with log utility would imply. In the capital-return version, it rises only by as much as the return to capital does in their model. These are two channels rather than a lower and upper bound; a complete model would add them, and the growth channel is much the larger.

I also vary what the Fed can see. It can know the true neutral rate; learn it by updating across the three scenarios as GDP data arrive; revise its estimate slowly; or, on top of slow revision, read the rise in AI-driven unemployment as slack.

What comes out of it

The neutral rate is most of the answer.

At the end of 2028 in the extreme scenario, the two versions of the neutral rate differ by about 11 percentage points (13.5 against 2.0 percent). Nothing else in the model moves the result a tenth as much. The growth version is the textbook answer: with growth near 15 percent, households only stop borrowing against a much richer future at very high real rates. The capital-return version implicitly assumes saving responds strongly, which the paper justifies by noting that compute is financed on world markets.

If the Fed could see the neutral rate, AI wouldn't be an inflation problem.

Because the Anthropic model's output is natural output, AI moves the target but opens no output gap. A Fed that tracks the true neutral rate with no lag delivers exactly the no-AI inflation path in every scenario. All the inflation in the charts comes from the Fed falling behind, either by design (smoothing its rate moves) or because it can't observe the neutral rate. So the monetary problem posed by AI is a measurement problem.

Reading AI unemployment as slack is the costly mistake.

Displaced workers raise unemployment while the neutral rate is rising. A Fed that reads unemployment through Okun's law sees slack and eases just when it should tighten. Even with the smaller capital-return neutral rate, this yields inflation of about 5 to 7 percent through 2032 in the substantial scenario, where natural unemployment rises only about a point. It is the real-time output gap error Orphanides documented for the 1970s, in a new form.

The data identify the scenario late.

Learning from GDP growth, the Fed puts only 18 percent on the extreme scenario by the end of 2027 even when it is true, and isn't 90 percent confident until late 2028. In the other two worlds, confidence comes in mid-2030. The scenarios barely diverge before 2027, which is the paper's own point.

Limits

Everything here is deterministic, with perfect foresight for households and firms, linear throughout, and coefficients chosen to be plausible rather than estimated. Rates in the double digits are outside the range where a linear model says much; the signs, orderings and timing are more trustworthy than the levels. What happens after 2030 is my assumption, not the paper's.

The clearest gap is the pair of neutral rate stand-ins, which should really be one. Moll, Rachel and Restrepo (2022), whose saving schedule the Anthropic model uses in reduced form, imply a neutral rate with both a growth term and a capital-share term; the reduced form drops the growth term. Chow, Halperin and Mazlish estimate that term's coefficient from long-run growth expectations across 59 countries. Anyone building this out properly would also want consumption rather than GDP growth in the saving decision (the investment build-out drives a wedge between them), a parameter for how open capital markets are, and a hazard rate for catastrophic outcomes, which raises the neutral rate. Run in reverse on inflation-protected Treasury yields, the same mapping would give the weight markets currently put on the fast scenarios.
